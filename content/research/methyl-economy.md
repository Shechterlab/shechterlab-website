---
title: 'The Methyl Economy of Cellular Proliferation'
date: 2026-06-28
summary: 'How dividing cells allocate one-carbon methylation potential across competing SAM-dependent reactions, and how that allocation determines proliferation, chromatin assembly, RNA processing, and genome stability.'
---

Cell proliferation may depend on a hidden **methyl economy** that allocates one-carbon resources across DNA, RNA, protein, lipid, and small-molecule methylation. Every dividing cell must solve this allocation problem while duplicating and packaging its genome.

One-carbon metabolism supplies **S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)**, the universal methyl donor, while methyltransferases consume SAM and generate the inhibitory product **S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH)**. The **SAM:SAH ratio** sets cellular methylation potential, but how dividing cells prioritize this resource among competing reactions remains an open question.

The lab provides two complementary entry points into this system that build on long-standing work on PRMTs, RNA processing, and chromatin assembly:

**Supply side.** We have shown that **glycine N-methyltransferase (GNMT)** responds to folate and tunes the SAM:SAH ratio, establishing GNMT as a physiological regulator of methylation potential.

**Demand side.** We have shown that **PRMT5 sustains S-phase histone production** by methylating newly synthesized histone H4; loss of PRMT5 disrupts histone gene regulation, depletes histones, and causes replication stress and genome instability. PRMT5-H4 methylation therefore provides a tractable entry point into a broader hierarchy of methylation-dependent processes that govern proliferation.

These findings lead to a central question: **how do cells allocate methylation potential across competing SAM-dependent reactions, and does this allocation determine how cellular systems fail when one-carbon metabolism becomes limiting?**

**Directions we are pursuing:**

- Defining a metabolic gate for genome packaging through PRMT5-H4 methylation, histone gene transcription, and histone locus body function.
- Building a methyl-spending hierarchy of dividing cells, using GNMT as a tunable rheostat together with methionine and folate limitation and isotope tracing, to rank DNA, RNA, protein, lipid, and small-molecule methylation outputs by sensitivity to methyl-donor stress.
- Testing whether distinct methyl-economy disruptions converge on common cell-state failures including impaired histone supply, altered RNA processing, membrane stress, replication stress, and genome instability, with GNMT, PEMT, and NNMT as primary regulated rheostats.

If this allocation logic holds, it would reveal a new layer of cellular control linking nutrition, one-carbon metabolism, chromatin assembly, RNA biology, membrane physiology, and genome stability, and would change how the field understands why dividing cells remain stable, adapt, or fail in cancer, aging, and metabolic disease.
