Roughly a third of adult acute myeloid leukemia is driven by a mutation in NPM1, a histone chaperone — the mutant protein (NPM1c) mislocalizes to the cytoplasm and rewires the cell's gene expression program. We found that the glutamyltransferase **TTLL4** modifies NPM1c and related acidic nuclear proteins, and that this glutamylation changes how they associate with chromatin and what the leukemic transcriptional program looks like. That makes TTLL4 something rare: a leukemia target discovered from the enzyme side first, with the biochemistry already worked out.

The work now runs on two tracks. One is causality — pinning down, step by step, how TTLL4 activity translates into chromatin behavior, transcription, and leukemia cell fitness. The other is translation: with the Gritsman (leukemia biology) and Kitamura (medicinal chemistry) labs at Einstein, we are pursuing TTLL4 as a first-in-class therapeutic for NPM1c-mutant AML. The founding study is [on bioRxiv](/publications/ttll4-aml-preprint-2025/), under revision at *Nature Communications*.
